    Did my message get through?

    People can't tell signal, internet and Bluetooth apart, and they don't need to. So the app never says online, connected, or how many people are near. It only says what happened to your message.

    Each message shows what happened to it

    The rest of the time, the app shows nothing about it

    Decide: what the app says about getting through

    Never sayOnline, offline, connected, signal, internet, network, people near you, hubs. No bars, no counts, no map of other phones. Why"Connected" makes people think they have internet. A count of people nearby gives them nothing to act on, and can feel like tracking. What they care about is whether their question got an answer. When to warnAs soon as the app knows a message can't get through: straight away if Bluetooth finds no other phones, or when a message has had no reply for 2 minutes. OpenWhen nothing gets through, should the app suggest going somewhere busier, such as a station or a high street? It is the one thing a person can do about it, but it hints at how the app works.

    What's going on?

    One big line, one fact under it, then three questions. Every answer is a fact with its source, or "Not known yet". No promises, no reassurance, no guessing.

    What can I find?

    People often don't know what to ask. So the screen shows what information exists, as six topics with a picture each. The picture works even for someone who reads little English. Each tile also says whether there is anything to find yet.

    Six topicsThe things people need first: water, a charged phone, food, family, a warm place, first aid.

    Tap a topicThe question becomes the title. Each place shows how far, what's there, and who said so.

    FamilyTwo ready messages, so nobody has to type. Mum gets it as a normal text message.

    Decide: the six topics

    ProposedWater, Charge phone, Food, Family, Shelter, First aid. They match the basic needs that rest centres and relief guides plan for first, and each has a picture people already know from signs. LanguageThe app uses the phone's language. Pictures come first and words second, so a topic is still clear to someone who reads little English. The answers can be in any language the server's model speaks.

    Can I get help?

    999 may not work, so the app never says it does. SOS sends a request for help with where you are, the same way questions travel. It asks once before sending, then says plainly whether it was delivered.

    Decide: who gets an SOS?

    ProposedEmergency services, and the family you chose. NeedsA real way to reach emergency services from the server. Until we have one, the bar says "your family", not "emergency services".

    Motion

    Short and plain: things fade in, bars fill, sheets slide. Motion only shows a real change, such as a message sent or news arriving. Nothing pulses, glows or loops.

    Icons

    Google's Material Symbols, Rounded and filled, free under the Apache 2.0 licence. The topic icons follow public signs: a glass for water, a battery for charging, a knife and fork for food. The iPhone build uses the matching SF Symbol, shown under each name.

    Words

    The test: read the screen aloud to a 9-year-old. If they ask what something means, or it sounds chatty, change it.

    1. Talk about the message, not the connection. "Delivered", "Not sent yet". Never "online" or "connected".

    2. State the fact. "Power is off. Since 2:20pm." Not "We're sorry, it seems…".

    3. No promises. Not "We'll tell you as soon as we hear". Just "Not known yet".

    4. Say only what people can use. No counts of phones or people, no technical words.

    5. Short words, short lines. One idea per line.

    6. Pictures first. For people who read little English.

    7. Never promise what may not work. Not "999 still works". Offer SOS.
